As the 2024 Cornell University gangrape case is being discussed again, people have been wondering about Cornell College’s own history with sexual violence on campus. Sexual harassment and violence is a global issue and Cornell College is no different. There is an ongoing backlog of thousands of Title IX complaints filed nationally. This isn’t just an individual school that is unable to protect its students. Study after study of sexual violence statistics say that the numbers are grossly underrepresented. More often than not,—as represented in the Cornell University case—it is the victim who ends up with more punishment than those who committed the heinous crime. Many victims in similar situations realize that their choices are to either pursue justice or graduate. 39% of student survivors take a leave of absence, transfer, or drop out ([Know Your IX][knowyourix]). Jane Doe was the one who left Cornell University and didn’t graduate, not the seven men who continuously violated her consent for hours on end.

In [Cornell College’s own Title IX language][cornellixarchive], they don’t recognize a victim, rather a “Complainant” and “Respondent.” By using this language, the college is already not assuming fault. “If you treat a victim and perpetrator the same, you will always be enabling the perpetrator and empowering them to have more power over the victim,” explains Nicole Bedra who spent a year observing the Title IX office at a large public university ([Jezebel][jezebel]). While in theory this might be a good practice to have an “impartial investigation,” a majority of the reported instances of sexual misconduct are ignored or give the benefit to the perpetuator because of this terminology. Rape, Abuse, & Incest National Network ([RAINN][rainn]) reports that one in four women undergraduates are victims of sexual assault. In one study, one in 10 male college students perpetuated sexual assault ([Kevin M. Swartout, PhD; Mary P. Koss, PhD; Jacquelyn W. White, PhD et al][smartpeople]). Whether because of understaffing in Title IX offices or other reasons, universities suspend one of every 12,400 students each year for sexual misconduct and expel one in 22,900 ([USA Today][usatoday]).

If you experience sexual assault, harassment, or violence you can report it to the Cornell College Reporting Form, call the Riverview Center’s 24-Hour hotline at [888-557-0310][riverview-hotline], your RA is a mandatory reporter and is trained to help you, or talk to a counselor at Ebersole. If you are a victim of rape, you should get a rape kit exam from a local hospital emergency room within 72 hours of the assault. You can decline any part of the exam that you are uncomfortable with. While your first instinct might be to shower, change, or clean up, do your best to keep as much evidence as you can to file a report. Once you are in a safe location, bag your clothes and go to an emergency room for medical assistance as soon as you can. While this won’t always get your case to move faster, there is a higher chance for a desired result if you have more evidence. If you would like to discuss the sexual misconduct reporting process, talk to the Title IX coordinators, Samantha Hebel Perkins or Doug Shannon in the Student Life Suite.

We aren’t Cornell University, but that doesn’t make this school different from the statistics of the mishandling of sexual violence cases.
