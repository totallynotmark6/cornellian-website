---
title: "Opinion: The Value of African American History Courses"
author: James Belding
pubDate: Wed Apr 29, 2026
---
As a history major, I love history. It can be complex, messy, and heartbreaking, but also heartwarming, empowering, and beautiful. That is because history is made by people; individuals, just like us. This history makes us and our society what it is today. Like the cliche goes, “those who don’t know their history are doomed to repeat it.” But there is more than just some cliche reason to learn and understand history. Our history makes us who we are. Not just state or even personal history—world history. Humanity is connected and the world’s history is our history. That’s also part of what makes the many history courses here at Cornell College so valuable.

In a traditional K-12 education, African American history is rarely a focus. How much do people really know about the Civil Rights movement outside of Rev. Dr. Martin Luther King Jr. and his I have a Dream speech? How many people are aware of the full title of The March on Washington; that its full title was: The March on Washington for Jobs and Freedom? How many people are aware of the connotations of the connection between employment and freedom? Even America’s history of the enslavement of millions of human beings is often discussed only briefly. The true complexity and importance of African American history and the humanity of the Black people involved has historically been neglected. The African American history courses at Cornell bring to light the history of America that students are often missing, and it is a history well worth remembering.

HIS 356, or African-Americans in U.S. History, is a course taught by Professor Phil Lucas that recontextualizes early American history up to the late 19th century with an appreciation of African American experiences during these times. The course combines the detailed books: Been in the Storm So Long by Leon F. Litwack and Roll, Jordan, Roll: The World the Slaves Made by Eugene Genovese with the first-hand accounts of Frederick Douglass in his autobiography, Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass, an American Slave. The research of Litwack and Genovese complement each other in various aspects. Their focuses are different, but the results of their research are complementary. Through these studies, students develop a greater appreciation and understanding of the realities of the Black experience from enslavement to emancipation.

Douglass’ autobiography is well known as a classic and for good reason: Douglass’ skill as a writer and his ability to concisely detail his experiences are powerful to this day. His story provides a first-hand account of what the reality of being enslaved was like. At times his accounts match with the evidence found by Genovese and Litwack. At other times Douglass presents a different perspective than what the two authors find. Understanding why this difference is there and what it means is difficult. Professor Lucas ably connects the various elements of the three texts and additional non-textbook sources together to present a fuller picture of the reality of the various experiences of Black people during the enslavement and the transformation into emancipation.

HIS 245, or Reel History: African Americans and Film, is a course offered by Professor Catherine Stewart. The course focuses on understanding the history of Black film and filmgoing from the 1930s until today. Several films are shown throughout the course and are paired with readings that help students understand the historical context. Students then discuss what they observed to develop a clearer picture of the significance of the film and the various elements of American history that they represent. The course is part of Professor Stewart’s “Reel History” series that looks at history through film—telling the untold and “reel” histories that people often are unaware of.

One of the films shown is Within Heaven’s Gates (1920), a film by Oscar Micheaux, an independent Black director from the silent film era that used all-Black casts to challenge racist depictions of Black people by white filmmakers. Micheaux’s films are largely lost, but what few remain are films that depict the virtue, determination, and humanity of Black Americans in a time when most of America would not accept this truth. Micheaux is the most well known of the silent film era, but he is not the only Black filmmaker of this time. The course also exposes students to other examples of Black filmmakers in the silent era and helps show the history that has long been hidden.

Other films present dramatically different depictions of Black people and race relations such as Sidney Poitier in the film, In the Heat of the Night (1967). The class is not limited in scope to silent film or films from the 1960s, and includes modern films that help show the complexities of intersectionality through the lens of Cheryl Dunye’s excellent film, The Watermelon Woman. Dunye wrote, directed, and starred in the film, and uses herself as a lens to explore being a Black queer woman. The film also presents the question of what history is saved and who has the ability to decide what history is worth saving.

The course ends with one of the most important documentaries ever made: Summer of Soul (...Or, When the Revolution Could Not Be Televised) (2021). The footage is of the 1969 Harlem Cultural Festival, an event that would be advertised as the Black Woodstock. The story of Woodstock is well known, but tragically the Harlem Cultural Festival was largely lost until only recently. Organized in the wake of the assassination of Rev. Dr. Martin Luther King Jr., the festival was held over five weekends and actually coincided with Woodstock. This footage was lost for many years and has long been forgotten by most people. This festival was a powerful scene in American history where the trauma, beauty, and value of Black music, culture, religion, and life encapsulated into an event that shows Black Americans and their history as they reckon with the end of the 1960s and the losses they have all endured.

These courses cover much more than what is presented here, but hopefully these few examples can serve as evidence that African American history courses have value at Cornell College and spark an interest in history as a whole.
