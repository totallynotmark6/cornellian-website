---
title: Truman Jordan on Changes in Liberal Arts at Cornell
author: Murray Page
pubDate: Wed Apr 29, 2026
---
Truman Jordan, Cornell College professor emeritus of Chemistry, is a long-time Mt. Vernon resident who came to Cornell in 1966 and retired in 2002. He has taught many different subjects, including medical ethics and computer science, but his main job was teaching chemistry. He was also the chair of the committee that studied and started the block plan at Cornell, and the chair of a committee in the 1970s that developed a plan for reducing the size of the faculty. Now, as he attends many Cornell sporting events, he calls himself an “outside observer” of what goes on on the Hilltop. He gets most of his insight from the students and faculty that he knows. From being involved in many years of Cornell history and being on the “hack and sack” committee, Truman has many thoughts and feelings on the cuts that recently took place within campus faculty.

Cornell was home to only 700 or 800 students when Truman first arrived in 1966. The faculty was therefore much smaller and the whole administration could fit into Old Sem. “If you wanted to find out what was really going on, you went to the secretary,” Truman said. Other differences during this time was that the dorms had house mothers and there was a dean of students, a dean of women, and a dean of men. The college also had four courses in which humanities faculty taught a broad selection. This program stopped before the block plan was in the talks. Today, the faculty is larger, but hasn’t doubled. However, the administration has more than doubled. College is a lot more expensive, not just at Cornell specifically, but around the nation. He points out how there have been lots of changes in education in general, as the breadth of knowledge has increased across the world but the amount of critical thinking has declined (especially in the US). He believes that there is an epidemic which was brought on by the internet, in which the whole country has now failed to understand the importance of education.

Truman attended a small liberal arts school that was similar to Cornell for his undergraduate because he didn’t want to go to a large university and get lost in the crowd. He had no idea what he wanted to do, but he knew it was the first time he ever felt intellectually at home. The experience was amazing, so he decided to pursue a PhD to make it possible for him to teach at a college like his. “It’s always been home to me,” Truman said about liberal arts institutions. As a now former chemistry professor, Truman believes in the importance of a liberal arts education. “STEM is just a narrow thing,” he said. He chose a liberal arts college for his undergrad because he wanted to be required to take a variety of courses. He still remembers the paper he wrote in a course his junior year that fulfilled his sociology requirement, even though the paper had nothing to do with his major of chemistry. He claimed that STEM is nothing without the liberal arts, and shares that he was embarrassed when he saw the announcement about the college’s decision to focus more on STEM and AI. “That’s not what a liberal arts college does, that’s what a trade school does,” he said.

The decision to switch to the block plan was a creative decision that was made in order to solve a budget issue. When chairing the committee for the block plan, Truman said his main job was to make sure that people didn’t get into fights. He distinctly remembers when the dean (now called the provost) and two other faculty came to the college with the idea of switching to the block plan. At the time, he figured they would kick it around for two to three meetings and then push it aside. “I couldn’t be more wrong,” he says now. After talking about it all fall they decided to take a trip with a few members of the faculty and student body to Colorado College to talk to people there about the block plan. After this, there was a lot of support from humanities and social sciences, but not much support for the sciences. This is because science courses (like labs) demand more in-class time, while the social sciences knew they would gain more students’ attention with this switch. He helped the chemistry department secure the promise of lab assistants which were necessary on this schedule. The vote then passed with a stipulation that they would review it again after a couple of years. They did, and some faculty—especially in the math department—left because they didn’t like the block plan which is harder on professors, but that is a sacrifice the college made to help with an issue they were facing.

With the recent non-STEM faculty cuts that Cornell has made, Truman believes that the college has decided to ignore certain branches of knowledge like the humanities which are the “heart and soul” of liberal arts. “In my mind it’s no longer a liberal arts college but it will continue to call itself that […] I fear the model we are heading to is a trade school,” said Truman. “The administration is heading in the direction of the college as a business by catering to students’ wants, instead of their educational needs.” In a letter sent to the Board of Trustees before their February meeting for the letter writing campaign initiated by the local American Association of University Professors chapter, Truman said, “this is by far the worst decision the college has made in the last 60 years.” He believes that these cuts show that the administration’s understanding of the liberal arts model is wrong and that from an outside perspective, it doesn’t appear that the trustees and leaders of the administration place nearly as much trust in the faculty as they used to.

While Truman was reporting on observations he made from the outside and hasn’t had any private conversations about the decisions with administrators, he is very connected to the Cornell community. He shares that he understands that the committee that was asked to deal with cutting faculty was asked to do it in a timeline that they felt like they couldn’t make. They asked the administration for the cuts to share the pain, but the opposite was done instead. He also understands that one professor was told that they were recommended to the president to be granted tenure at the end of the year, but they were fired before the end of the year came. He said the ability to analyze and discuss issues with other people and reach a logical agreement about them is something that is lacking at Cornell today, compared to when the creative decision to switch to the block plan was made. Truman disagrees with the decisions that were made and said that “everyone involved in the decision making had good intentions, I just don’t agree with what they did.”

For Truman, teaching chemistry was his entry into the liberal arts college world, but he shares that everyone who teaches at Cornell is fascinated by the subject they teach. This is why the decision to cut a number of faculty was so devastating to them, because it disrupts the model that they have built their life around. “I have never seen faculty morale at Cornell as low as it is now,” he said. Overall, about those who made the cuts, he said, “I’m not mad at them, I’m just disappointed.” People make mistakes all the time, but they need to course-correct now and recommit to the liberal arts model. For the students, he wants to make sure that this discussion continues next year. He doesn’t want this to be the last thing he sees on it. He doesn’t want this decision to be forgotten.
