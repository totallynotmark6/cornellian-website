---
title: It Starts With Consent
author: Edwin A Rivera Nieves
pubDate: Oct 7 2026
---

Hey, don’t get confused. This is Cornell College, not Cornell University. But whether we're talking about Cornell College or Cornell University, one thing shouldn't change: **consent matters.**

Consent is something that should exist everywhere in the world. It does not depend on the place, the time, someone’s ethnicity, race, gender, or preferences. Everyone has boundaries, and everyone deserves to have those boundaries respected.

Recently, a sexual assault case involving a Cornell University student brought this conversation back into the spotlight. The student was reportedly pressured into using drugs and alcohol before being raped by 7 male students at the Chi Phi fraternity house over several hours on October 19–20, 2024\. Although this incident did not happen at Cornell College, it is still something worth talking about. We may not be able to change what happened or the outcome of that situation, but we can learn from it.

At the same time, the conversation shouldn't turn into saying that men are dangerous or that women are helpless. Sexual assault and consent are not simply “men’s issues” or “women’s issues.” Sometimes, we make conversations about sexual assault so focused on gender that we forget the bigger point. We start asking what someone was wearing, how much they drank, who they were with, or what they could have done differently. But those questions can distract us from the simplest part of the conversation: **Did that person consent?**

Consent is not about trying to read someone’s mind. If someone says no, you stop. If someone seems uncomfortable, you check in. If someone is too intoxicated to make a decision, you do not take advantage of that. And if someone changes their mind, you respect it, which means that consent can be taken away as fast as it was given. This also means understanding that everyone’s boundaries can be different. Something that one person is comfortable with might make someone else uncomfortable. That does not make either person wrong. It just means that communication matters.

This is especially important in college. We meet new people, make friends, go to parties, date, and experience situations we may not have been exposed to before. We are all still learning how to communicate with each other. That means we also have a responsibility to listen when someone tells us where their limits are.

And this responsibility belongs to everyone. We should be able to talk about consent without immediately turning the conversation into an argument about who is to blame based on gender. We should be able to talk about someone’s choices without using them to justify someone else’s actions. Most importantly, we should be able to recognize that respecting a boundary is not complicated or extraordinary. It is simply respect.

The Cornell University case did not happen here. But that does not mean we cannot learn from it here. What we can do is decide what kind of community we want Cornell College to be. A community where “no” is respected. A community where people can change their minds. A community where being under the influence is never treated as an invitation. A community where respecting someone’s boundaries is simply expected.



Consent is not a men’s issue. It is not a women’s issue.

**It is a human issue.**
